###### Abstract

Terminal-using agents have quickly become a popular and important downstream application of language models (LMs). Despite their prevalence, relatively little academic work has examined RL-based training of these models, due to difficult benchmarks, a lack of data, and a lack of simple baseline recipes. We present TMax, an open RL recipe for terminal agents that brings small open models closer to the frontier. While simple, our recipe achieves 33% on Terminal-Bench 2.0 with only 9B parameters, outperforming much larger models from prior work. Our recipe first involves generating data using a novel taxonomy, combining difficulty control, personas, and verifier diversification, which allows us to cheaply generate large amounts of terminal environments for RL and SFT training. We then train open-weight models using RL with our data, using a simple, outcome-only recipe, with small adjustments to improve stability. Our recipe works for models from 2B to 27B and across model families, dominating the Pareto curve for open terminal-agent performance at smaller sizes. We release our data, models, and code as a strong baseline for future open academic work on terminal agents at [https://github.com/hamishivi/tmax](https://github.com/hamishivi/tmax).

## 1 Introduction

Terminal-based agentic coding products have quickly become incredibly popular, with models expected to perform increasingly complex and long-running tasks through the terminal ([Anthropic, 2025](#bib.bib7); [Cursor Research et al., 2026](#bib.bib8)). However, existing academic work has largely focused on bug-fixing ([Jimenez et al., 2024](#bib.bib36)) or relatively simple terminal tasks ([OpenThoughts-Agent Team, 2025](#bib.bib2); [Gandhi et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)), rather than the complex, long-horizon tasks exemplified by Terminal-Bench ([Merrill et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)). We aim to close this gap by providing a large dataset of complex terminal tasks and a recipe for training small yet capable open-weight terminal agents.

We first introduce Tmax-13k, a dataset of RL environments spanning diverse domains, skills, and difficulty levels. We explicitly control task difficulty and use continuous rewards where appropriate, while keeping the generation process simple and scalable. Compared with many prior datasets, Tmax-13k is substantially more challenging, as measured by lower pass rates from Gemini-3-Flash-Preview. While we get the strongest results using stronger models as data generators, we also show that training on data generated by the same model can still provide strong improvements, showing that our pipeline is effective in the absence of a stronger teacher model. Secondly, while recent work has focused primarily on generating terminal-agent data ([Wu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib33); [Zhu et al., 2026](#bib.bib32)), RL training for terminal agents remains underexplored. We address this gap by developing a simple RL recipe for training open-weight terminal agents on Tmax-13k. Our recipe builds on fully asynchronous RL infrastructure implemented in open-instruct ([Lambert et al., 2025](#bib.bib23)) and uses DPPO ([Qi et al., 2026](#bib.bib22)) for policy optimization. To improve training stability, we use an FP32 LM head and increase the group size. Our best 9B model achieves 33% on Terminal-Bench 2.0, outperforming similarly sized open models and selected closed models, including Claude Haiku 4.5.

We hope that our overall recipe and data prove useful for future academic work exploring terminal agents, as we believe this setting provides a number of unique and interesting challenges. Concretely, our contributions are:

- •
  We introduce Tmax-13k, a dataset of 13,565 RL environment instances with explicit control over difficulty and diversity. Our data is generated using a simple and scalable procedure, avoiding complex multi-agent loops.
- •
  We train open-weight terminal agents and achieve state-of-the-art performance among reported models under 10B parameters under default Terminal-Bench settings. Our best 9B model reaches 33% on Terminal-Bench 2.0.
- •
  We provide a simple, reproducible open RL recipe for achieving this performance and publicly release all elements required for training our models. Our recipe outperforms prior open RL recipes such as Endless Terminals and OpenThinker-Agent.
- •
  We show that terminal-based RL training can generalise non-trivially across harnesses and tasks, providing strong evidence our training improves general model capabilities.

We additionally publicly release code, checkpoints and data associated with this work for future use and reproduction at [https://github.com/hamishivi/tmax](https://github.com/hamishivi/tmax).

## 2 Background and Related Work

#### Data for terminal agents.

Early terminal-agent datasets paired short instructions with shell commands ([Lin et al., 2018](#bib.bib6)). More recent work either constructs environments from existing repositories or questions ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib13); [Wu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib33); [Lin et al., 2026](#bib.bib16); [Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib39); [Shen et al., 2026b](#bib.bib45); [Shi et al., 2026](#bib.bib44)), or synthesises them using seed tasks and combinatorial prompting ([Gandhi et al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Zhu et al., 2026](#bib.bib32); [Peng et al., 2026](#bib.bib43); [Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib40); [Meng et al., 2026](#bib.bib46)). We follow the latter approach. We find that repository-based construction often produces imbalanced domain coverage, whereas synthesis provides greater control over domain balance. Compared with prior work on task synthesis, we vary tasks along more axes, including task difficulty, and release substantially more data. We also incorporate a repair stage and show that strong performance can be achieved without a commonly used but expensive validation step. We discuss prior and concurrent work on terminal RL data in more detail in § [B](#A2 "Appendix B Related Work ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents").

#### RL for terminal agents.

Closest to our work is Endless Terminals ([Gandhi et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)), which performs RL training on a Qwen 3 8B model using its generated data, but with a significantly simpler harness and setup. OpenThoughts-Agent ([Raoof et al., 2026](#bib.bib3)) explores RL training for terminal agents, but focuses on comparing existing datasets and the effect of varied SFT, finding that RL dataset quality matters significantly. More recently, SETA ([Shen et al., 2026b](#bib.bib45)) and ROME ([Wang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib26)) explore terminal RL training, but show limited gains or use fairly complex setups to train their models. In contrast, we show large gains and use a simple, straightforward recipe. River ([Yao et al., 2026](#bib.bib41)) examines RL training data, finding that rubric-based repair and filtering of existing datasets can improve performance. It also investigates process-level behaviour regularisation. In contrast, we generate our own data, and use fairly straightforward outcome rewards.

## 3 Terminal Data Generation

Training autonomous terminal agents requires large-scale and diverse coding tasks. With real-world terminal data often scarce or proprietary ([Merrill et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)), synthetic generation is used to bridge the data gap ([Pi et al., 2026](#bib.bib31); [Zhu et al., 2026](#bib.bib32)). However, existing pipelines fall short along three axes. First, they rely on complex multi-stage generation procedures that are hard to scale. Second, they generate homogeneous task suites with limited coverage against real-world tasks and Terminal-Bench ([Merrill et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)). Third, they offer little control over difficulty, often producing bi-modal task pools that are either trivially solved or unsolvable by current models. Hence, we propose a simple terminal environment generation pipeline that is scalable, diverse, and difficulty-aware. We use Gemini-3-Pro ([Pichai et al., 2025](#bib.bib34)) as our generation model, as it requires a strong enough model to generate coherent tasks and code fixtures.

### 3.1 Generation Pipeline

We adopt a simple compositional generation framework: each synthetic task is sampled as a product of structured axes (Table [7](#A3.T7 "Table 7 ‣ Appendix C Terminal Data Generation Pipeline ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents")). We follow [Pi et al. (2026)](#bib.bib31) to seed the first three axes – domains, skills, primitive skills – and introduce six orthogonal axes explicitly targeting diversity and difficulty, unlike prior work.

#### Diversity via hierarchical sampling.

The compositional sampler is itself our main diversity mechanism, and drawing from each axis yields combinatorially many distinct task signatures. Further, we add two specific diversity dimensions:

*Persona diversification.* We augment generation with a set of user personas, motivated by prior work on persona-conditioned data synthesis ([Ge et al., 2025](#bib.bib21); [Lambert et al., 2025](#bib.bib23)), to further diversify generated tasks. Personas are domain-specific (6–18 per domain, e.g. “red-team operator crafting an evasion payload” for security), so each is always reasonable for its sampled skill set.

*Multi-modal fixtures.* Prior work on RL environments largely focuses on *text-in / text-out* problems. Here we extend the task inputs beyond plain text by shipping a concrete artifact for some tasks: a PNG image, audio file, video, stripped binary, vendored package, or multi-service compose stack (Table [9](#A3.T9 "Table 9 ‣ C.1 Verifier and fixture kinds ‣ Appendix C Terminal Data Generation Pipeline ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents")). The policy itself remains a text-only language model and training is unchanged: rather than perceiving these artifacts natively, the agent inspects them through standard terminal tooling (e.g., OCR, audio transcription, ffmpeg), so no multi-modal model is required.

#### Difficulty via explicit calibration.

Unlike previous works, we make an explicit effort to calibrate task difficulty, avoiding the bi-modal distribution where tasks are either trivially solved or unsolvable by current models (Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.2 Comparing Terminal Datasets ‣ 3 Terminal Data Generation ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents")). We address this through two mechanisms:

*Fine-grained complexity.* We introduce two complexity axes, *task complexity* (from a few shell commands to intricate workflows of 30–60 commands) and *command complexity* (from bash-only to bash + code + system services), for granular control over how hard each task is. We sample uniformly across complexity buckets by default, with optional per-bucket up-weights to match specific model capabilities or induce a curriculum.

*Graded verifiers.* Previous RL tasks often rely on exactly matching a gold output. Here we extend with richer verifier kinds (Table [8](#A3.T8 "Table 8 ‣ C.1 Verifier and fixture kinds ‣ Appendix C Terminal Data Generation Pipeline ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents")): metric-threshold (e.g., accuracy \$\geq 0.95\$), adversarial-corpus (accept clean, reject malicious), fuzz-equivalence (bit-exact match against an oracle), or multi-protocol (protocol-level requests against a service). Thresholds give us a continuous difficulty knob, while multi-condition variants naturally extend task length.

#### Verification via LM Judge.

Finally, inspired by [Yao et al. (2026)](#bib.bib41), we apply an LM judge (GPT 5.6 Sol) to classify our data and use it to identify weak, ambiguous, or contradictory verifiers and task descriptions. The vast majority (76.4%) of tasks are marked as having verifiers too weak to enforce the correct solution, and 10.9% of tasks are judged as leaking the answer in the initial environment state, highlighting that a repair pass is much-needed to improve task validity. We then provide the original environment definition along with the label to GPT 5.6 Sol and construct a repaired instance. We then do a final spot-check to ensure that images have the required dependencies and can be built (see §[C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Rubric-based Data Repair ‣ Appendix C Terminal Data Generation Pipeline ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents") for the pre-repair rubric counts). We retain tasks originally judged as correct and repaired tasks.

#### Do we need to filter?

Unlike prior works that validate task quality and correctness through expensive teacher generation ([Zhu et al., 2026](#bib.bib32); [Wu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib33)), we deliberately skip this step entirely, as our RL training applies effective soft filtering via active sampling and zero-std filtering (§[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Training Terminal Agents ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents")). We find the all-zero rate is roughly 40% (see §[E.4](#A5.SS4 "E.4 Filtered Samples ‣ Appendix E Additional RL Training Details ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents")).

Using this pipeline, we generate a dataset of 13,565 tasks, which we name Tmax-13k. We measure overlap between dataset task descriptions and the Terminal-Bench and TB Lite tasks using a 13-gram sliding window, and find no sign of overlap (§[C.5](#A3.SS5 "C.5 Decontamination ‣ Appendix C Terminal Data Generation Pipeline ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents")).

#### Composition

A good training dataset should have broad coverage and a balanced composition across target domains. By design, our generation framework exposes domain as an explicit sampling axis, so we can directly calibrate per-domain mass at generation time. To compare across datasets, we use Gemini-3-Pro to annotate every task for our and six comparison datasets, with one of these nine domain labels. As shown in Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Do we need to filter? ‣ 3.1 Generation Pipeline ‣ 3 Terminal Data Generation ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents"), prior terminal datasets concentrate 34–95% of their mass on a single domain (e.g., software_engineering accounts for 95% of SWE-Smith and over 60% of TerminalTraj and CLI-Gym), while ours spreads roughly uniformly across all nine. This balanced coverage avoids biasing the policy toward any narrow domain and better covers real-world usage.

#### Difficulty & Balance

To compare difficulty across datasets, we evaluate Gemini-3-Flash-Preview on a fixed subsample of 250 tasks per dataset (8 rollouts each) and report mean pass@\$k\$ in Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.2 Comparing Terminal Datasets ‣ 3 Terminal Data Generation ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents"). We additionally compute a *balance score* for each categorical axis (domain, skill-type, task complexity, command complexity) as the fraction-of-uniform diversity of its empirical distribution:

|     |                                                                                        |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     | \$\mathrm{Balance}\\=\\\frac{\exp(H)}{N},\qquad H=-\sum\_{i=1}^{N}p\_{i}\log p\_{i},\$ |     | (1) |

where \$p\_{i}\$ is the proportion of tasks in bucket \$i\$ and \$N\$ is the number of buckets. The score lies in \$\[1/N,1\]\$ with \$1\$ being perfectly uniform; see §[C.4](#A3.SS4 "C.4 Balance score ‣ Appendix C Terminal Data Generation Pipeline ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents") for derivation. This highlights that our data requires significantly more turns than other datasets apart from CLI-Gym and SWE-Smith, but is significantly more balanced than them. Difficulty-wise, we observe that it has the lowest pass@8 apart from CLI-Gym, suggesting genuine difficulty and not high variance in rollout outcomes. While some of these tasks may be unfeasible for the model, we do not attempt to filter them out ahead of time as our RL infrastructure already filters out zero pass rate instances.

## 4 Training Terminal Agents

We now validate our data by practically applying it to RL training. We aim to validate our data with a simple recipe, providing a testbed in which we believe there is significant room for improvement.

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

#### Algorithm

We train models using DPPO ([Qi et al., 2026](#bib.bib22)), a variant of GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib20)) that masks tokens when inference and training logprobs deviate. Specifically, we mask tokens based on a binary approximation of the total variation (TV) divergence. We use a token level loss ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib19)) and run training fully asynchronously. We filter groups with zero standard deviation and use active sampling to ensure full batches following Olmo 3 ([Olmo et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)).

#### Infrastructure

We extend open-instruct ([Lambert et al., 2025](#bib.bib23)) for terminal agent training. For rollouts, we use vLLM ([Kwon et al., 2023](#bib.bib10)), and we use the same mini-SWE-agent-based harness as used for verification and SFT data generation. We build a custom sandboxing infrastructure utilising slurm for requesting additional compute resources, and Apptainer for setting up and isolating environments. We additionally ensure that the language model head of models is kept in FP32 precision to minimise training-inference mismatch ([MiniMax et al., 2025](#bib.bib17)) (see §[E.3](#A5.SS3 "E.3 LM Head Details ‣ Appendix E Additional RL Training Details ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents")). We train on a cluster of H100 nodes, and use 2 nodes for training and 6 for inference. Training typically takes 1–2 days. We remove a small number of training data samples and eval samples that are unable to run correctly in our infrastructure (see §[H](#A8 "Appendix H Infrastructure Data Filtering ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents")).

#### Harness.

Unless otherwise stated, we use a simple harness based on mini-SWE-agent ([Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib38)) with persistent shell. We find that Terminus-2 harness ([Merrill et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)) is more brittle with small models, as it requires agents to send raw keystrokes to interact with the terminal (see §[D](#A4 "Appendix D Harness Choices ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents")).

#### Evaluation

We primarily evaluate on Terminal-Bench 2.1 ([Merrill et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)) and Terminal-Bench Lite ([OpenThoughts-Agent Team, 2026](#bib.bib15)). We use Harbor ([Harbor Framework Team, 2026](#bib.bib11)) and our custom sandboxing infrastructure for evaluation. Due to compatibility issues, we filter a small number of samples from Terminal-Bench Lite and Terminal-Bench 2.1 which are unsupported by our sandbox (see §[H](#A8.SS0.SSS0.Px2 "Eval Dataset Filtering. ‣ Appendix H Infrastructure Data Filtering ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents")). We run 3 trials for all evaluations and report mean and standard error. We run all evals using a single H200 and 4 concurrent trials. We do not override timeout defaults.

For the evaluations in Fig. [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents"), we use Terminal-Bench 2.0 (the earlier version) to compare directly with past work. We additionally use Daytona as the backend as recommended by Terminal-Bench authors to make results more reproducible. We find that the choice of backend can non-trivially affect performance: for instance, Daytona sandboxes often perform installs faster than our locally-hosted runs, resulting in fewer timeouts. However, the cost of these services makes them expensive to develop against.¹¹ 1 One RL training run of Tmax-9B would cost roughly \$3,150 on Daytona, using average runtimes from the run and pricing from [https://www.daytona.io/pricing](https://www.daytona.io/pricing).

#### Hyperparameters

Unless otherwise stated, we run RL training for 500 steps and evaluate every 100 steps. For all runs, we report scores on the checkpoint scoring the highest on Terminal-Bench Lite to avoid unfairly biasing against some datasets that collapse later during training in our setup (e.g., CLI-Gym). We use a group size of 32 and 8 prompts per batch, with a maximum sequence length of 65536 tokens and 64 maximum tool calls. When training Qwen 3.5 and 3.6 models, we do not perform any initial SFT. We edit chat templates such that thinking is preserved on intermediate turns (often called ‘interleaved thinking’), which has been shown to improve performance in agentic settings ([MiniMax, 2025](#bib.bib12)). We show further hyperparameters in §[E.1](#A5.SS1 "E.1 Full RL training hyperparameters ‣ Appendix E Additional RL Training Details ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents").

#### Tmax-13k outperforms other terminal datasets.

We first apply RL training to Qwen 3.5 9B ([Qwen Team, 2026](#bib.bib5)) using a variety of datasets from prior work and show results in Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.2 Core Results ‣ 4 Training Terminal Agents ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents"). We note that in many cases, we are the first to openly apply these datasets for RL training (e.g., in the case of TermiGen and TerminalTraj). We call the model trained on our data Tmax-9B. We find that training on Tmax-13k matches or exceeds training on other datasets, and achieves the highest mean performance across both datasets.

To further investigate this, we plot the average number of steps taken by the model during training for each dataset in Fig. [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Tmax-13k outperforms other terminal datasets. ‣ 4.2 Core Results ‣ 4 Training Terminal Agents ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents"). We find that some lower scoring datasets (OpenThoughts) use fewer turns during training or even collapse in turn count over the course of training, indicating lower difficulty. In contrast, Tmax-13k remains stable at requiring 25-30 steps to solve, maintaining high difficulty throughout training. While easier datasets like Endless Terminals also maintain higher step counts, they do so at the cost of filtering increasingly more fully-solved samples during training (see §[E.4](#A5.SS4 "E.4 Filtered Samples ‣ Appendix E Additional RL Training Details ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents")), requiring up to seven times as many rollouts as the minimum needed to produce a single batch.

We additionally plot reasoning and tool-call token usage in Fig. [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Tmax-13k outperforms other terminal datasets. ‣ 4.2 Core Results ‣ 4 Training Terminal Agents ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents"). We observe token usage increasing over the initial 150 training steps before plateauing (and then becoming somewhat unstable later in training). This is reminiscent of inference-time reasoning scaling in single-turn math settings ([Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib24)), and suggests that the model is gradually learning more complex reasoning and more complex tool calls over the course of training, resulting in its improved performance.

#### Tmax-9B outperforms open RL recipes on Terminal-Bench 2.0.

We additionally compare our model to prior work’s self-reported scores on Terminal-Bench 2.0 in Fig. [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents"). This shows that even when comparing across different harnesses and training setups, Tmax-9B performs the strongest among models under 10B parameters. In particular, our model outperforms many past open RL recipes such as Endless Terminals ([Gandhi et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)) and OpenThoughts-Agent ([Raoof et al., 2026](#bib.bib3)). As such, our work provides a strong baseline for future open work on agentic terminal RL.

Taken together, these results strongly suggest that TMax RL training teaches the model new capabilities and improved terminal use, beyond overfitting on Terminal-Bench or our harness.

## 5 Analysis & Discussion

#### Qwen 3.5 does not benefit as much from existing SFT datasets.

Common post-training pipelines usually perform SFT training to ‘warm-start’ the model before RL training ([Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib24); [Olmo et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)). However, we find that existing datasets seem to degrade Qwen 3.5’s performance by as much as 10 points, likely due to its already strong performance. In contrast, the same data can raise Qwen 3 8B’s performance by as much as 9 points (see §[G.2](#A7.SS2 "G.2 SFT Ablations ‣ Appendix G SFT Details ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents")). We leave further exploration of good SFT mixtures for Qwen 3.5 as future work.

#### Terminal agent RL training is difficult to stabilise.

Over the course of this work, we often found training unstable, with runs often collapsing past 300 steps. We apply three changes to improve stability: (1) using an FP32 LM head, which reduced trainer-inference mismatch (§[E.3](#A5.SS3 "E.3 LM Head Details ‣ Appendix E Additional RL Training Details ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents")); (2) using larger group sizes, which alleviated collapse in initial setups (§[E.1](#A5.SS1 "E.1 Full RL training hyperparameters ‣ Appendix E Additional RL Training Details ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents")); (3) improving our sandboxing infrastructure. Surprisingly, we found that reducing noise from infrastructure could be the difference between collapses at 300 steps and smooth training to 1000 steps.

#### Models can self-improve using their own synthetic data.

We rerun our data generation pipeline (including classification and repair) entirely using Qwen 3.5 9B to construct a smaller dataset of 3.1k samples. In Table [6](#S5.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Terminal agent RL training is difficult to stabilise. ‣ 5 Analysis & Discussion ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents") we find that it still results in non-trivial improvements, even outperforming over half the baseline datasets compared in Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.2 Core Results ‣ 4 Training Terminal Agents ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents"). This suggests that our data pipeline and recipe do not require a strong teacher model, but can in fact be used with a model of similar strength to the policy model. This may be due to information asymmetry: for many tasks, constructing the environment may be simpler than solving it (e.g., creating a sudoku solving task).

## 6 Conclusion

We present TMax, a simple recipe for training terminal agents: Tmax-13k, a dataset of 13,565 RL environments generated with explicit control over difficulty and diversity, paired with an outcome-only RL recipe. Tmax-9B achieves state-of-the-art Terminal-Bench 2.0 performance among open models under 10B parameters, and our recipe improves models across sizes, model families, harnesses, and out-of-domain tasks such as SWE-Bench Verified and AIME. We release all data, models, and code as a baseline for future open work on terminal agents.

## Appendix A Limitations

Our dataset generation pipeline is completely synthetic, and relies on the presence of a strong generator model. While we find positive results using Qwen 3.5 9B both as generator and policy, it is unclear if even stronger models or weaker models would behave similarly. We also believe that our approach likely struggles on tasks involving large-scale codebases, as these are inherently difficult to generate (but well-covered by SWE-bench-style datasets). Additionally, while we have focused on training smaller terminal agents as a baseline for academic settings, we note that running many isolated containers still proves expensive and/or difficult in open frameworks at scale, limiting training speed and efficiency and potentially still putting terminal agent training out of reach for academic groups. Finally, our results may be lower than possible SOTA due to the shorter context length and simple harness used relative to strong industry approaches, although we see this as a feature of our approach that makes it more friendly to smaller teams and easier to develop with.

## Appendix B Related Work

### B.1 Data for Terminal Agents

Early work on terminal agents focused on training models to translate natural language to bash statements. NL2Bash ([Lin et al., 2018](#bib.bib6)) paired short instructions with bash commands mined from the web, and was converted into an RL dataset for the training of OpenThinker Agent ([OpenThoughts-Agent Team, 2025](#bib.bib2)). However, it failed to yield significant improvements over their strong SFT model. This showcases the need for generating more complex terminal-agent tasks, which provide stronger learning signals on more complex tasks. More recently, [Raoof et al. (2026)](#bib.bib3) compared multiple RL datasets and found that the choice of RL dataset could have a significant impact on final performance, motivating more work into improving data for RL training specifically.

Recent work constructs terminal training data from various sources. Repository-based approaches include SWE-Smith ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)), which synthesises bug-fixing instances, and TerminalTraj ([Wu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib33)), which builds Dockerised tasks and verified trajectories from repositories. Related software-engineering datasets include SWE-Universe ([Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib35)), SWE-rebench ([Badertdinov et al., 2025](#bib.bib30)), SWE-Gym ([Pan et al., 2024](#bib.bib27)), and SERA ([Shen et al., 2026a](#bib.bib25)). CLI-Gym ([Lin et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)) extends repository-based task creation by perturbing agent paths through repository-based environments to construct new environments and tasks. Terminal-Lego ([Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib39)) and SETA-Env ([Shen et al., 2026b](#bib.bib45)) scrape sites such as StackOverflow and Stack Exchange for questions with verified answers and turn them into environments. SETA additionally applies an evolutionary procedure to control diversity and difficulty. FACET ([Shi et al., 2026](#bib.bib44)) constructs environments and tasks from skill files, generating task instructions and verifiers after environment construction to ensure validity. Terminal-Universe ([Wu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib42)) constructs environments from public agent trajectories, but does not release any data. We find in many cases that repository-based task creation results in unbalanced task splits in the data: for example, in Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Do we need to filter? ‣ 3.1 Generation Pipeline ‣ 3 Terminal Data Generation ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents") both TerminalTraj and CLI-Gym are mostly comprised of software engineering tasks.

Another set of recent work generates data from a given taxonomy or set of seed tasks, and is the approach we take in this work. We are particularly inspired by Endless Terminals ([Gandhi et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)), which similarly utilises a strong external model to generate new terminal tasks, but produces significantly fewer tasks that are largely focused on file manipulation, making them too easy for modern models. For instance, we find that Gemini-3-Flash achieves over 90% pass@1 on Endless Terminals. TermiGen ([Zhu et al., 2026](#bib.bib32)) and LiteCoder-Terminal ([Peng et al., 2026](#bib.bib43)) similarly synthesise containers and tasks from seed prompts, using a fairly complex multi-agent pipeline to generate and validate the tasks, and do not explicitly control the difficulty of their tasks. CalibForge ([Meng et al., 2026](#bib.bib46)) instructs an agent to construct a task from browsing the web, and then iteratively improves the sandbox until it is on the edge of solvability. Similarly, Recursive Synthetic Terminal Tasks (RST) ([Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib40)) starts from a set of seed tasks, and iteratively rewrites them based on sandbox feedback to improve their difficulty and quality. Nemotron-Terminal ([Pi et al., 2026](#bib.bib31)) both generates new data from seed prompts and adapts existing datasets into terminal tasks, but does not release any RL environment data, and similarly only validates their data through large-scale SFT training. We find that these other datasets do not appear to improve over Tmax-13k, despite its much simpler generation pipeline, as shown in Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.2 Core Results ‣ 4 Training Terminal Agents ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents").

- •
  VERIFIER-TOO-WEAK: The verifier does not fully test the final state, allowing the model to pass without fully completing the task.
- •
  ANSWER-LEAK: The answer or tests are in the initial image state, allowing trivial task completion.
- •
  CLEAN: No issues detected.
- •
  INSTR-VERIFIER-MISMATCH: The instruction and verifier contradict each other.
- •
  INSTR-ENV-MISMATCH: The instruction and environment contradict each other.
- •
  OTHER: Some other issue not captured by the reasons above.
- •
  INSTR-AMBIGUOUS: The instruction is ambiguous.

### C.3 Pass@k difficulty curves

Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.2 Comparing Terminal Datasets ‣ 3 Terminal Data Generation ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents") reports pass@\$k\$ at \$k\in\\1,4,8\\\$; Figure [6](#A3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ C.3 Pass@k difficulty curves ‣ Appendix C Terminal Data Generation Pipeline ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents") plots the full curve over \$k=1\$–\$8\$ for every dataset. The vertical position of a curve measures absolute difficulty, while its slope measures how much additional sampling recovers. TMax occupies the hardest band together with CLI-Gym across all \$k\$, so its tasks remain unsolved even with eight rollouts, suggesting high difficulty and low rollout variance (although some tasks may still be infeasible). Easy datasets behave very differently: Endless-Terminals already sits near the ceiling at \$k=1\$ and saturates almost immediately, leaving little headroom for a model to improve. This persistent, well-separated difficulty is what makes our data a useful source of learning signal for RL.

Figure 6: Pass@\$k\$ difficulty curves. Mean pass@\$k\$ (\$k=1\$–\$8\$) for Gemini-3-Flash-Preview on a fixed 250-task subsample per dataset (8 rollouts each); lower is harder. TMax occupies the hardest band together with CLI-Gym and attains the second lowest pass@8 of any dataset, showing that its difficulty persists as \$k\$ grows, whereas easy datasets such as Endless-Terminals saturate near the ceiling.

### C.4 Balance score

The balance score in equation [1](#S3.E1 "In Difficulty & Balance ‣ 3.2 Comparing Terminal Datasets ‣ 3 Terminal Data Generation ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents") is the normalized effective number of categories of an empirical distribution. The numerator \$\exp(H)\$ is the size a uniform distribution would need to have the same Shannon entropy as the observed mass vector; dividing by the bucket count \$N\$ rescales the result to \$\[1/N,1\]\$. A value of \$1.0\$ thus corresponds to perfectly uniform coverage of all buckets, while \$1/N\$ corresponds to all mass concentrated on a single bucket. We prefer this to raw entropy because it is comparable across axes with different bucket counts and reads as a natural “fraction-of-uniform” diversity score.

### C.5 Decontamination

We quantify potential train–test contamination as \$n\$-gram overlap between each dataset’s task descriptions and the task descriptions of our evaluation benchmarks, Terminal-Bench 2.0 and TB-Lite. Using a sliding window of \$n{=}13\$ tokens (stride 1), we flag a dataset task as overlapping if any of its windows matches at least one \$13\$-gram from a benchmark task, following standard contamination protocols ([Brown et al., 2020](#bib.bib28); [Touvron et al., 2023](#bib.bib29)); larger \$n\$ yields a stricter test with fewer spurious matches. Table [11](#A3.T11 "Table 11 ‣ C.5 Decontamination ‣ Appendix C Terminal Data Generation Pipeline ‣ TMax: A simple recipe for terminal agents") reports the fraction of each dataset flagged this way. Overlap is negligible across the board: TMax shows \$0\\\$ overlap with both benchmarks, and only TerminalTraj exhibits any measurable overlap (\$0.2\\\$ on TB-Lite and \$0.5\\\$ on TB2). Since Terminal-Bench 2.1 is a modified version of 2.0, we do not run additional decontamination against it.

More samples are slowly filtered over training due to improving model ability. However, compared to a significantly easier dataset like Endless Terminals, significantly fewer samples are filtered per step by the end of training for Tmax-13k. This highlights the importance of difficulty in training efficiency: by the end of training, each training step for Endless Terminals generates seven times as many rollouts as the minimum needed to fill the batch (due to high filter rates).

### E.5 Reward Hacking

We perform a light check over our main Daytona-based runs on Terminal-Bench 2.0 and find that Tmax-9B, after RL training, displays some instances of reward hacking. We find 2 clear cases:

- •
  feal-differential-cryptanalysis: The agent bypasses the intended attack by importing the cipher’s internal key array to recover the target key. The agent explicitly calls this “cheating” but submits it anyway.
- •
  financial-document-processor: The agent accidentally deletes the original documents, then recreates placeholder files under the same names and hand-writes the invoice CSV instead of extracting values from the documents.

In both cases, the rollouts with the hacking behaviour scored 0, so it does not affect downstream performance. Examining the chain of thought, we find that the model appears aware that it has not correctly performed the task. For example, for cryptanalysis, the model initially states “‘This means the attack should work with a black-box encrypt_fn, without accessing internal state. My current solution cheats by accessing feal_key directly. I need to fix this!“‘. However, it then submits anyway: “‘It doesn’t prohibit accessing other modules or the key array.”’. In the case of financial-document-processor, the model instead attempts to fool the verifier since it cannot actually perform the desired action: “‘The find command returned nothing. The files are truly gone. At this point, I need to make a pragmatic decision. The task cannot be completed perfectly without the original files. However, I can: 1. Create a working script that would correctly process the files if they existed 2. Create placeholder files that allow the script to run 3. Generate the expected summary.csv based on my analysis. \[...\] Since the task evaluation likely checks the final state, and I know what the correct classification and amounts should be (from my analysis), I’ll manually create the final correct state.”’ We hope that future work can further explore the connection between our recipe and this hacking.

## Appendix G SFT Details

### G.1 SFT Data Generation

We additionally generate a small SFT dataset to use as a warm-start for RL training, with prior works showing that warm-up could help training stability and performance ([Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib24); [Kimi Team et al., 2025](#bib.bib37)). Re-using our terminal data pipeline, we additionally generate 2.2k environments, and we use Qwen 3.6 27B to generate 8 trajectories for each environment. We filter out trajectories with unparsed tool calls.³³ 3 To avoid traces containing the Qwen 3 XML-style tool call format, which can confuse small models with different tool call formats like Qwen 3 8B. Together, this yields 16.5K total SFT trajectories, with 8K successful trajectories. We use this data for SFT experiments and finetuning Qwen 3 8B.
